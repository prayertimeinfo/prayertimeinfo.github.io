Why Prayer Times Shift Every Day — Astronomy in Plain Words

Short notes on the astronomy behind the daily timetable.

Open any monthly prayer timetable and the times slide a little every day. Fajr creeps earlier, then later; Maghrib stretches into the evening, then pulls back. It is not an error — it is the Earth doing its job.

Reason 1: The Earth's axis is tilted

The Earth spins on an axis tilted about 23.4 degrees. As we orbit the sun, that tilt points us toward or away from it, giving us seasons — and swinging sunrise and sunset times by over an hour across the year. Maghrib and Fajr ride this swing directly.

Reason 2: The orbit is not a perfect circle

The Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical, and it moves faster when closer to the sun. Combined with the tilt, this produces the equation of time: the difference between clock noon and solar noon, swinging about ±16 minutes through the year. Dhuhr — defined by the sun's actual peak — follows solar noon, not your watch, so it drifts too.

Reason 3: Twilight is a gradient, not a line

Fajr and Isha depend on how far the sun is below the horizon, and that descent happens at different speeds in different seasons. In summer the sun drops steeply; near the equinoxes it slides more gradually. So the gap between sunset and Isha, or between Fajr and sunrise, stretches and shrinks through the year.

What this means for your timetable

A good timetable is just these three effects computed precisely for your location and calculation method. That is why location matters so much — shift a few hundred kilometers and every time moves. For a working example, the Jeddah prayer timetable shows the monthly drift clearly: scan a full month and you can watch all three effects play out in the numbers.

Once you see the pattern, the daily shift stops feeling random. It is astronomy, rendered as a schedule.
